\begin{abstract}

We introduce MultiIFEval, a large‑scale instruction‑following benchmark that spans 305
languages. Starting from the English IFEval dataset \cite{zhou2023ifeval}, we translate
and localise each prompt–response pair into 305 target languages using an LLM-based
pipeline with Wikipedia-grounded localisation. We create a manually translated Danish
variant of the dataset and conduct human expert validation to assess quality differences
between machine-translated and manually-translated Danish. We evaluate models on both
machine-translated and manually-translated Danish, as well as on Estonian \cite{dorkin2025ifeval-et}
and French \cite{alhajar2024ifeval-fr} variants from prior work. Finally, we evaluate
multiple models across all 305 languages to establish a comprehensive baseline for
cross-lingual instruction following capability.

\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Instruction following evaluation has become central to developing practical AI systems
capable of understanding and executing natural language commands. The release of IFEval
\cite{zhou2023ifeval} established a standardised framework for measuring how well models
can follow verifiable instructions in English, providing a rigorous benchmark that goes
beyond traditional task completion metrics. However, IFEval's English-only scope reveals
a critical limitation: as large language models are increasingly deployed across diverse
linguistic contexts, evaluation benchmarks confined to English fail to capture
performance disparities that arise from multilingual instruction following.

The gap between monolingual and multilingual evaluation has only partially been addressed
by recent work. M-IFEval \cite{li2025xifbench} expanded to 3 languages, XIFBench
\cite{he2024multi} covered 6 languages, and Multi-IF \cite{he2024multi} reached 8
languages. More recently, Marco-Bench-MIF \cite{zeng-etal-2025-marco} extended coverage
to 30 languages. While these efforts represent important progress, they collectively
cover fewer than 1\% of the world's approximately 7,000 languages, leaving the vast
majority of linguistic communities without instruction-following evaluation resources.
This limitation is particularly acute for low-resource languages, where model behaviour
may diverge significantly from high-resource settings due to differences in training
data availability, tokenisation quality, and linguistic structure.

To bridge this gap, we introduce \textbf{MultiIFEval}, a benchmark that expands IFEval
to 305 target languages, representing an order-of-magnitude increase in multilingual
coverage. Our methodology leverages large language models to both translate and localise
every English prompt–response pair from the original IFEval dataset into each target
language. Crucially, our localisation pipeline grounds translations in Wikipedia
articles in the target language, ensuring that cultural references, proper nouns, and
contextual examples are adapted appropriately rather than translated literally. The
resulting dataset spans high-resource languages such as Spanish, Mandarin, and Arabic,
as well as low-resource languages including Xhosa, Quechua, and Samoan, providing an
unprecedented testbed for evaluating multilingual instruction following at scale.

In addition to machine-translated versions, we provide a manually translated and
localised Danish subset curated by native speakers and validated by domain experts. This
dual approach enables systematic comparison between machine-translated and
human-translated instruction-following benchmarks, allowing us to assess whether
translation quality meaningfully impacts model evaluation outcomes—an aspect largely
unexplored in prior multilingual benchmark work. We also incorporate manually translated
Estonian \cite{dorkin2025ifeval-et} and French \cite{alhajar2024ifeval-fr} variants
from recent independent efforts, enabling cross-lingual analysis across multiple
European languages with human-validated translations.

Our main contributions are methodological:

\begin{itemize}
\item We present a 305-language instruction-following benchmark, expanding multilingual
  coverage from the prior maximum of 30 languages to 305, spanning languages across all
  inhabited continents and representing diverse language families and resource levels.

\item We create a manually translated and localised Danish variant, enabling direct
  comparison between machine translation and human translation quality for instruction
  following evaluation, with expert validation to assess the validity of
  LLM-based translation pipelines.

\item We develop a methodology for LLM-based translation with Wikipedia-grounded
  localisation, providing a reproducible approach for adapting instruction-following
  benchmarks to new languages while preserving cultural and contextual appropriateness.

\item We establish an evaluation framework for cross-lingual instruction following that
  will be applied across all 305 languages, enabling systematic analysis of how model
  performance varies across language families, resource levels, and translation methods.
\end{itemize}


\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\paragraph{IFEval}
% Original English benchmark
\cite{zhou2023ifeval} introduced IFEval, the first systematic benchmark for instruction-following evaluation in large language models. IFEval comprises approximately 500 prompts with verifiable constraints that can be checked programmatically, enabling objective binary evaluation of whether models successfully follow instructions. The benchmark covers four constraint types: content inclusion/exclusion, formatting requirements, length constraints, and punctuation rules. While IFEval established a rigorous framework for measurable instruction-following assessment, its scope is limited to English, leaving open the question of how models perform when following instructions in other languages.

\paragraph{M-IFEval}
% 3 languages, translated + language-specific
\cite{m-ifeval2025naacl} extended IFEval to three languages---French, Japanese, and Spanish---creating M-IFEval. This work evaluated both translated versions of the original English prompts and language-specific instructions designed natively in each target language. Their evaluation of 8 LLMs revealed notable cross-lingual performance gaps, with models generally performing worse on non-English instructions even when capabilities appeared comparable on English tasks. M-IFEval demonstrated that instruction-following quality does not transfer uniformly across languages, motivating broader multilingual evaluation.

\paragraph{XIFBench}
% 6 languages, constraint-based taxonomy
\cite{li2025xifbench} introduced XIFBench, expanding coverage to 6 languages with a fine-grained constraint taxonomy organised into five categories: Content, Style, Situation, Format, and Numerical constraints. XIFBench comprises 558 instructions designed to test specific constraint types, enabling diagnostic analysis of where models fail. Their taxonomy provides a structured framework for understanding instruction-following weaknesses, though coverage remains limited to a small set of predominantly high-resource languages.

\paragraph{Multi-IF}
% 8 languages, multi-turn
\cite{he2024multi} further expanded multilingual instruction following to 8 languages with Multi-IF, introducing the critical dimension of multi-turn interaction. Rather than evaluating single-turn instructions, Multi-IF assesses how well models follow sequences of constraints across multiple conversational turns. Their findings showed that models struggle significantly more with multi-turn constraints compared to single-turn instructions, with performance degrading as the number of constraints accumulates across turns. This work highlighted that real-world instruction following often requires maintaining constraints over extended interactions, not just single prompts.

\paragraph{Marco-Bench-MIF}
% 30 languages, localisation vs translation
\cite{zeng-etal-2025-marco} represents the largest prior effort with Marco-Bench-MIF, covering 30 languages. Crucially, this work distinguished between machine translation and localisation, adapting prompts to reflect cultural context and local knowledge rather than translating literally. Their evaluation of over 20 LLMs uncovered substantial disparities: 25--35\% performance gaps between high-resource and low-resource languages, and 7--22\% gaps between machine-translated and properly localised prompts. These findings underscore that translation method meaningfully impacts evaluation outcomes and that resource disparities remain a central challenge.

\paragraph{Gap}
% Motivating our 305-language scale
Despite this progressive expansion, prior multilingual instruction-following benchmarks have reached a maximum of 30 languages, representing less than 0.5\% of the world's approximately 7,000+ languages. MultiIFEval addresses this gap by covering 305 languages---a tenfold increase over the previous maximum---providing unprecedented coverage across diverse language families, resource levels, and geographic regions. This scale enables systematic analysis of instruction-following capabilities across the full spectrum of linguistic diversity, rather than a narrow subset of high-resource languages.


\section{Dataset Generation}
\label{sec:dataset-generation}

The MultiIFEval dataset is generated via prompting a large language model in a zero-shot
fashion, for each sample of IFEval. The prompt contains both the instruction to
translate and localise each English sample. To reduce the risk of hallucination when
localising the examples, as well as to increase the quality of the translations, we
include for each IFEval sample a randomly chosen Wikipedia page in the target language.
We use structured generation to ensure that the output is of the correct format. The
full prompt can be found in Figure~\ref{fig:prompt}.

The structure of the generated \texttt{kwargs} deviate from the input \texttt{kwargs},
and the reason for this is that the language model we used for generation did not
support dictionaries as values in the JSON schema used in structured generation, so the
is merely a rephrasing of the keyword arguments to obey this constraint.

We use Gemini-3-flash-preview~\cite{google-ai2026gemini-3-flash-preview} for dataset generation with zero-shot
prompting. Structured generation via JSON schema ensures consistent output format and
facilitates automated validation. For each target language, we provide a randomly
selected Wikipedia article in that language as contextual grounding. This serves two
purposes: (1) it reduces hallucination by anchoring the model in authentic
language-specific content, and (2) it provides cultural context that informs
localisation decisions beyond literal translation.

Localisation---adapting content to a specific culture and locale---is essential for
instruction-following benchmarks. Direct translation often preserves source-culture
references (e.g., holidays, currencies, place names, cultural practices) that feel
foreign or unnatural to native speakers. Our localisation approach asks the model to
adapt prompts so they read as if originally authored in the target language, replacing
Anglocentric examples with culturally appropriate equivalents.

\begin{verbatim}
You are a professional translator from English to {target_language} (language code:
'{target_language_code}').

Here is an instruction-following example in English:

<example>
{"prompt": <prompt>, "instruction_id_list": <instruction_id_list>, "kwargs": <kwargs>}
</example>

You need to translate the example to {target_language}. This means the following:

1. The `prompt` should be translated to {target_language}. You should localise these to
   {target_language} and its country's culture.
2. The `instruction_id_list` should remain exactly the same.
3. The keys in the kwargs dictionaries should be completely unchanged, and the values
   should _only_ be changed if they contain English words or phrases, which would need
   to be changed to the equivalent in {target_language}.

Here is an example of some text written in {language.name}:

<{target_language_code}_example>
{wikipedia_article_in_target_language}
</{target_language_code}_example>

You should return the translated example in JSON format, with the following structure:

- `new_prompt` (str): The translated prompt.
- `new_instruction_id_list` (list[str]): The translated instruction ID list.
- `new_kwargs` (list[list[dict]]): The translated keyword arguments.

Here the `new_kwargs` must be a list of the same length as the
`new_instruction_id_list`, containing lists of dicts, each with keys `name` and `value`,
where `name` is the name of the keyword argument and `value` is the value of the keyword
argument. Each list of kwarg dicts should be the keyword arguments for the corresponding
instruction ID in the `new_instruction_id_list`.
\end{verbatim}

\subsection{Manual Danish Translation}
\label{sec:manual-danish-translation}

To validate the quality of machine-translated samples, we created a manually translated
and localised Danish subset of IFEval. Two native Danish speakers on our research team
translated all prompts, with careful attention to localisation: cultural references
were adapted to Danish contexts (e.g., replacing American holidays with Danish
traditions, converting measurements to metric, using Danish place names and currency).
The manual translation serves as a gold standard against which we evaluate the machine
translation quality in Section~\ref{sec:comparison}.

Danish was selected for manual translation for two reasons: first, it is well-represented
in training corpora, making it an informative test case for machine translation
quality; second, having native speakers on our team enabled rigorous manual validation
without external contracting costs or delays.

\subsection{Machine Translated Dataset}
\label{sec:mt-dataset}

We generated machine-translated versions of IFEval for 305 languages using the prompt
template shown in Figure~\ref{fig:prompt}. For each language, we selected a
contemporary Wikipedia article at random to provide contextual grounding. The
structured generation pipeline enforces strict preservation of \texttt{instruction\_id\_list}
values, ensuring traceability to original English samples. The \texttt{kwargs} are
reformulated as lists of name--value pairs to comply with the JSON schema constraints
of the generation model.

Quality control is enforced at multiple levels: (1) the JSON schema guarantees valid
output structure; (2) we validate that all instruction IDs from the source are present
in the output; (3) we verify that kwargs retain their original keys, with only values
modified where localisation is appropriate. Due to model limitations, a small subset
of languages yielded empty or malformed outputs; these are excluded from the released
dataset. The final MultiIFEval corpus covers 305 languages with varying sample counts
depending on source availability and generation success rate.


\section{Experiment Setup}
\label{sec:evaluation}

We evaluate a range of language models on MultiIFEval to assess instruction-following capabilities across languages. Our evaluation focuses on decoder-only models, which represent the dominant architecture for instruction-following tasks, while including encoder models for comparative analysis.

\paragraph{Models}
We evaluate multiple decoder-only models, including Llama-3.1-8B variants and other open-weight models of comparable scale. For comparison, we also include encoder-based models to examine architectural differences in multilingual instruction following. All models are evaluated in a zero-shot setting without task-specific fine-tuning.

\paragraph{Evaluation Approach}
Each model is evaluated on each language variant of MultiIFEval using zero-shot instruction following. Models receive instructions in the target language and must generate responses that satisfy the specified constraints. We do not provide examples or demonstrations, ensuring evaluation measures inherent multilingual capabilities rather than in-context learning.

\paragraph{Metrics}
Following the original IFEval methodology, we measure constraint satisfaction using binary pass/fail scoring per instruction. A model receives a pass only if its response satisfies all constraints specified in the instruction. We report both per-instruction accuracy and aggregate scores across constraint types.

\paragraph{Cross-lingual Analysis}
Our analysis examines performance patterns across language families, writing systems, and resource levels. We compare high-resource languages (e.g., French, German) against low-resource languages (e.g., minority African and Indigenous languages) to identify disparities in model capabilities. We also analyze whether performance correlates with language family membership or geographical proximity.

\paragraph{Comparison with MultiWikiQA}
As optional analysis, we examine correlation between MultiIFEval performance and MultiWikiQA scores, using the latter as a proxy for general language understanding. This comparison helps distinguish instruction-following specific capabilities from broader multilingual competence.

\section{Comparison of Machine vs Manual Translations}
\label{sec:comparison}

A key question in multilingual benchmark construction is whether machine translation produces evaluation datasets comparable to manual human translation. We analyze quality differences between our machine-translated datasets and manually-translated variants across three languages: Danish (our new manual translation), Estonian \cite{dorkin2025ifeval-et}, and French \cite{m-ifeval2025naacl}.

\paragraph{Manual Validation Methodology}
For Danish, we conduct expert validation comparing machine-translated and manually-translated instructions. Native Danish speakers with expertise in NLP evaluate both versions for: (1) naturalness and fluency, (2) preservation of constraint semantics, (3) cultural appropriateness of localized content, and (4) overall usability for evaluation purposes. Validators rate each instruction on Likert scales and provide qualitative feedback on problematic cases.

\paragraph{Cross-language Analysis}
We reference existing manual evaluations of Estonian \cite{dorkin2025ifeval-et} and French \cite{m-ifeval2025naacl} IFEval variants to contextualize our Danish findings. While each manual translation employed slightly different validation protocols, all three provide expert assessment of machine translation quality for instruction-following benchmarks.

\paragraph{Expected Analysis}
Our analysis will examine whether machine translation introduces systematic biases or errors that affect evaluation validity. We will compare model performance rankings on machine-translated versus manually-translated datasets to assess whether translation method affects relative model comparisons. We hypothesize that machine translation performs adequately for high-resource languages but may introduce artifacts in low-resource languages or for culturally-specific content.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We present MultiIFEval, an instruction-following benchmark spanning 305 languages, enabling evaluation of multilingual models at unprecedented scale. Our dataset combines machine-translated coverage across all languages with manually-translated, expert-validated variants for selected languages.

MultiIFEval addresses a critical gap in multilingual NLP evaluation by providing standardized instruction-following assessment across diverse languages and writing systems. We expect this resource to support research on multilingual instruction tuning, cross-lingual generalization, and equitable model development across language communities.

Future work includes expanding manual translations to additional languages, developing culturally-adapted instructions beyond translation, and conducting comprehensive evaluation across the full model ecosystem. We plan to release MultiIFEval publicly to support the broader research community.
